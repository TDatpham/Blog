1. About Typo.css

p { color: red }

The purpose of Typo.css is to build the most suitable web typography for Chinese reading while unifying browser typesetting effects.

Current situation and how to address it:

Typesetting is a troublesome issue # Appendix 1 that requires careful design, but this design is often overlooked by visual designers. Front-end engineers are more likely to notice such problems but are not in a position to make changes. Because rendering differs in different browsers across multiple OSs, modifications require a lot of time for regression testing, making changes more difficult. The Resets commonly used by us, such as Yahoo, Eric Meyer, and Alice base.css, do not take Chinese typesetting into full consideration. What Typo.css aims to do is solve the problem of Chinese typography.

Key Points and Challenges in Chinese Typesetting

In Chinese typesetting, many standards in HTML4 have taken semantics into account. However, from a visual perspective, it is difficult to achieve certain effects with CSS alone, such as the emphasis mark (example: emphasize here). In HTML4, the underline tag (<u>) was deprecated, but it was reintroduced in HTML5. Typo.css also provides corresponding solutions based on actual situations. There are two important points we need to pay attention to:

  1. Semantics: Whether the usage and style of semantics are consistent with Chinese typesetting
  2. Presentation: Whether the font, size, and scaling in various browsers match the expected typography

Example 1: The Analects — Xue Er (First Chapter)

Author: Confucius ( September 28, 551 BC – April 11, 479 BC )

Introduction

"Xue Er" is the title of the first chapter of *The Analects*. Each chapter in *The Analects* is generally named after the first two or three characters of its first passage. The "Xue Er" chapter contains 16 passages, covering various topics. Key ideas include "I reflect on myself three times a day"; "Be economical and love others, govern the people at the proper time"; "The essence of ritual lies in harmony"; as well as moral concepts such as benevolence, filial piety, and integrity.

Original Text

Confucius said: "Isn’t it a pleasure to learn and practice constantly? Isn’t it a joy when friends come from afar? If others do not know me and I do not resent it, is that not the mark of a gentleman?"

Translation

Confucius said: "Isn’t it a great joy to learn something and practice it regularly? Isn’t it delightful when like-minded friends come from distant places? If people do not understand me, yet I do not resent them, is that not being a virtuous gentleman?"

Analysis

The renowned Song Dynasty scholar Zhu Xi held this passage in high regard, saying it is "the gateway to the Dao and the foundation of virtue." These three sentences are very familiar to many people. Traditional interpretations are: after learning, one often reviews and practices, which brings happiness, and so on. The three sentences each convey a separate idea, and the sentences are not necessarily connected. However, some scholars argue that such interpretations do not align with the original meaning. They point out that "learning" here does not mean studying but rather one’s doctrine or proposition; "time" does not mean often, but the era or society; "practice" does not mean review but means application or adoption. Furthermore, the three sentences are not isolated but interconnected. The meaning is: If my doctrine is adopted by society, I will be very happy; if not, but many friends agree with my doctrine and come to discuss it with me, I will also feel happy; and even if society does not adopt it and people do not understand me, I will not resent them. (See *Qilu Journal*, Issue 6, 1986). This explanation is internally consistent and reasonable, and readers may consider it when interpreting this passage.

In addition, regarding the interpretation of the phrase "If people do not know [me] and I do not resent [them]", some argue that the phrase lacks an object — what exactly do people not know? At the time, Confucius had a specific context and did not need to explicitly state it, but it left a puzzle for later generations. Some suggest this sentence follows from the previous one: when friends from afar ask me for advice and I explain it to them, yet they still don’t understand, I do not resent them. Thus, "people do not know" means "people do not understand what I explained". However, this interpretation seems somewhat forced.

In short, this passage advocates taking pleasure in learning and achieving a state of not resenting others' ignorance, reflecting Confucius’s emphasis on tireless learning, diligent teaching, self-cultivation, and strict self-requirement. These ideas appear throughout *The Analects* and help deepen the understanding of the first chapter’s content.

You may want to create a perfect <hr /> line, despite the fact that there will never have one

La Racheforcauld said: "Few things are impossible in themselves; and it is often for want of will, rather than of means, that man fails to succeed". You just need to follow the browser's behavior, and set a right margin to it. It will work nicely as the demo you're watching now. The following code is the best way to render typography in Chinese: 


/* Titles should be closer to the content and distinct from other blocks, so margin values need to be optimized accordingly */
h1,h2,h3,h4,h5,h6 {
    line-height:1;font-family:Arial,sans-serif;margin:1.4em 0 0.8em;
}
h1{font-size:1.8em;}
h2{font-size:1.6em;}
h3{font-size:1.4em;}
h4{font-size:1.2em;}
h5,h6{font-size:1em;}

/* Modern typography: ensure white space between blocks/paragraphs */
.typo p, .typo pre, .typo ul, .typo ol, .typo dl, .typo form, .typo hr {
    margin:1em 0 0.6em;
}
